CHAPTER 02 // MOTIVATION

Some ideas stay with you until you
finally do something about them.

Where wildlife photography curiosity met computer science, and a dormant ambition found its catalyst.

Some projects begin with a problem statement assigned on a briefing sheet. Others begin with a personal interest that stubbornly refuses to remain just a weekend pastime.

For me, WILDHACK 2K26 was where two of my deepest pursuits — computer science and wildlife — finally converged. I have always been fascinated by artificial intelligence, computational geometry, and the quiet power that emerges when software tackles real-world friction. At the same time, I am an avid wildlife photography enthusiast.

When you spend dawn hours sitting in a safari jeep in the teak forests of Central India, tracking movement through a telephoto lens, you begin to see wildlife differently. Every Bengal tiger is not just a representative of Panthera tigris; it is a living individual with a distinct identity, an intricate behavioral history, and a unique biometric fingerprint etched across its flanks in high-contrast melanistic stripes.

I had been thinking about wildlife re-identification for quite some time. The concept of turning raw camera-trap photographs into verifiable individual identities was something I talked about, researched in academic papers, and repeatedly postponed because of semester workloads and data scarcity.

Then, scrolling through LinkedIn, the announcement for WILDHACK 2K26 appeared: an offline hackathon organized in Ballarpur, Chandrapur, in direct collaboration with the Tadoba-Andhari Tiger Reserve.

A single thought crossed my mind, clear and unavoidable: If not me, then who? It was time to stop postponing the idea and start building.

CHAPTER 03 // THE GATHERING

First offline hackathon. Twenty-four hours.
A problem that mattered to us.

Arriving at Ballarpur Institute of Technology with laptops, cameras, and a shared commitment to Indian conservation.

WILDHACK 2K26 took place on 16–17 October 2026 at the Ballarpur Institute of Technology in Bamni, Ballarpur, located in the Chandrapur district of Maharashtra — a stone’s throw from the renowned tiger landscapes of Tadoba-Andhari.

The competition began with an initial round open to engineering teams from across the nation. The total number of participating teams was kept confidential by the organizers, but when the shortlists were published, only the top 30 teams had been invited to travel to Chandrapur for the final offline round.

Our team, HackOPs, was among those selected. For all four of us, this was our first-ever offline hackathon. The atmosphere when walking into the BIT campus was electric: students from different states unpacking desktop rigs, configuring local GPU environments, comparing notes, and preparing to build non-stop for 24 hours.

What brought the four of us together was not the pursuit of another bullet point on a résumé. It was a shared conviction that technology should serve the living forest.

Walking into an offline hackathon is very different from submitting code remotely. You look across the room and see dozens of talented peers tackling conservation issues with drones, acoustic sensors, and satellite imagery.

We knew our objective had to be crisp: we did not want to show a slide deck of hypothetical ideas. CHAPTER 04 // THE PROBLEM

What if recognising a tiger was
only the beginning?

The official challenge asked us to identify individual wild animals across changing viewpoints, lighting, and quality. The real complexity was far more subtle.

The official problem statement was framed in a single clear sentence:

“Re-Identifying Individual Wild Animals — Identify individual animals, even when the viewing angle, lighting, distance, or image quality changes.”

Official Problem Statement // WILDHACK 2K26

At first glance, non-specialists often conflate species detection with individual re-identification. Modern object detectors like YOLO or Faster R-CNN can locate a tiger in a camera-trap frame in less than 20 milliseconds with overwhelming accuracy. But finding a tiger tells a forest manager almost nothing about population health.

Imagine a remote camera trap anchored to a sal tree in the Moharli range. At 06:39 AM, it captures a tiger crossing a firebreak. Three days later, another camera seven kilometers away in the Kolsa sector captures what might be the same tiger — but this time photographed at twilight, from a sharp three-quarter rear angle, walking uphill, with its lower torso obscured by dry bamboo fronds.

Is it the same individual? If it is, the observation proves that the animal has crossed a territorial buffer corridor. If it is not, an unrecorded individual has entered the range.

This is why the problem is scientifically profound: a tiger's skin is non-rigid. When it walks, turns, or crouches, fur folds and stretches. A simple pixel-to-pixel comparison or direct convolutional feature embedding fails because viewpoint variations easily overwhelm individual identity signals.

To solve this, a system must separate where the tiger is positioned from the immutable geometric topology of its stripe markings.

CHAPTER 05 // ARCHITECTURE

We didn't want to stop at an idea
or a presentation.

The technical heart of WildID: a simulation-driven computer vision pipeline engineered to extract identity from raw forest pixels.

When building under a 24-hour clock, you immediately encounter the central bottleneck of computer vision: annotated training data. High-resolution photographs of wild tigers labeled with individual IDs, exact bounding boxes, and keypoints are virtually nonexistent in public academic repositories due to anti-poaching security protocols.

Instead of giving up or resorting to generic classification, we asked an unconventional question:

“What if we could automate data collection through simulated wildlife environments?”

We used Blender, its Python scripting interface (bpy), and GroveScape procedural foliage generators to create a virtual dry deciduous forest. Through automated local batch scripts, we rendered approximately 10,000 synthetic wildlife images — systematically varying camera elevation, solar angle, shadow dapple, and tiger body postures.

Simulation gave us something pure: ground-truth geometric certainty. We knew the exact camera distance, the exact visible body coordinates, and the exact stripe positions for every generated frame.

Using this synthetic foundation alongside real wildlife images, we structured WildID as a multi-stage modular pipeline:

STAGE 01
Tiger Segmentation
Isolating the animal from dense forest undergrowth and background foliage using high-precision boundary masks.
> 95% Prototype Bench
STAGE 02
Stripe Pattern Isolation
High-pass spatial frequency filtering to eliminate ambient lighting gradients and isolate melanistic stripe bifurcations.
> 80% Detection Rate
STAGE 03
Pose Estimation
Skeletal keypoint detection establishing which body regions (left flank, right flank, shoulder, croup) are visible in the frame.
> 70% Keypoint Align
STAGE 04
Fur-Carpet Normalization
Unwrapping extracted markings onto a canonical 2D planar template, transforming non-rigid fur into a standardized biometric map.
Planar Vector Unwrap
STAGE 05
Identity Matching
Vector embedding similarity search comparing candidate stripe graphs against the central observation database.
> 70% Match Retrieval

It is critical to be intellectually honest here: synthetic data is an accelerator, not a substitute for the wild.

While 10,000 synthetic renders gave us a controllable testbench to debug the pipeline's geometry and code linkages, a computer-rendered leaf does not match the messy, unpredictable optics of an infrared trail camera triggered in heavy rain. We knew that validating the pipeline against genuine field datasets would be the ultimate hurdle.

CHAPTER 06 // THE CRUNCH

Building is one thing.
Making everything work together is another.

The visceral reality of the 24-hour sprint: fighting latency, pruning fragile code, and pushing through exhaustion at 2:58 in the morning.

The romantic notion of a hackathon is a burst of effortless inspiration. The reality is an unrelenting sequence of practical compromises made under sleep deprivation.

By 10:00 PM on Friday night, the initial excitement had given way to pure problem-solving. Each of us had an individual component running locally: Vaishnav was generating batch renders; Lokesh had built the GIS mapping coordinates; Soham was testing the Flask/JS interface; I was tuning the stripe high-pass filter thresholds.

Then came the real battle: getting five distinct subsystems to communicate seamlessly in real time.

When we chained the segmentation mask output into the pose estimator, memory leaks caused local processes to freeze. When the pose estimator passed coordinates to the fur unwrapper, edge-case camera angles produced matrix inversions that crashed the matching loop.

At 02:58 AM, we made the call that saved our project: we stripped away complex speculative microservices and unified the pipeline into a lean, locally executing workflow.

We stopped chasing theoretical perfection and focused entirely on architectural robustness. If a user dragged in a new image, the system had to execute every step reliably, draw the reticles, project the matching correspondence lines, and plot the sighting on the map without stuttering.

By 07:00 AM on Saturday morning, as the sun broke over the Ballarpur campus trees, the pipeline executed end-to-end for the first time. We were exhausted, running on cold chai, but we had a living system.

Beyond Identification: Actionable Conservation Telemetry

We showed the judges that WildID was designed to turn isolated photographic sightings into longitudinal ecological intelligence:

  • [01] Territory Roaming Polygons: Generating convex hull roaming territories across Tadoba sectors (Moharli, Kolsa, Tadoba Basin) to trace home range stability.
  • [02] Ecological Resource Correlation: Mapping sighting density against perennial water sources, including the Andheri River basin, to detect drought-driven migrations.
  • [03] Role-Based Conversational RAG: A local AI assistant enabling range officers to query: “Which camera captured T-12 last week, and has its range overlapped with T-09?”

Seeing the judges lean forward as the live Leaflet map updated its markers, and watching our pipeline extract real tiger stripes in under two seconds, was an unforgettable feeling. We weren't reading bullet points from a screen; we were showing something that genuinely functioned.

CHAPTER 08 // CRITICAL EVALUATION

The most useful feedback was about
what we hadn't solved yet.

The judges appreciated our system's scalability. Then they delivered the crucial critique that gave WildID its true future.

The evaluation did not stop at polite compliments. The judging panel included professionals with deep backgrounds in forest administration, wildlife research, and computer engineering.

They were impressed by the architecture. They praised how modular the pipeline was, noting that the coordinate unwrapping and stripe matching could, in principle, be adapted to leopards, snow leopards, or zebra species with minimal structural refactoring.

Then, one of the judges addressed the core of our technical methodology:

“Your simulation approach is clever for rapid prototyping. But to be trusted by forest departments, your models must be trained and validated on real, messy camera-trap photographs.”

Judges' Panel // WILDHACK 2K26 Technical Review
WHAT WAS PRAISED // ARCHITECTURAL STRENGTH
Scalability & Multi-Species Adaptability

The judges recognized that WildID avoided hardcoded shortcuts. The clean separation between segmentation, pose geometry, planar vector unwrapping, and identity querying creates a modular pipeline that can scale beyond a single forest reserve.

WHAT WAS CHALLENGED // THE HARD TRUTH
Synthetic Data vs. Camera-Trap Reality

Blender simulations produce clean polygons and predictable lighting. Real camera-trap imagery suffers from low dynamic range, intense infrared flare, motion blur, rain streaks, and partial body occlusions. A model must be trained on this messiness to be scientifically dependable.

Hearing that feedback was the most valuable moment of the entire hackathon.

It would have been easy to feel defensive. But the judges were entirely correct. A scientific tool cannot rely on synthetic approximations when the subjects are living endangered animals whose conservation relies on absolute precision.

The judges didn't dismiss our work — they held it to the highest standard of real-world conservation biology. They handed us the exact roadmap we needed to pursue.

CHAPTER 10 // THE ROADMAP

The 24 hours ended.
The problem didn't.

Transitioning from a 24-hour sprint to an ongoing research initiative: what it will take to make WildID truly field-ready.

TWILIGHT CHRONICLE // 18:28 PM SPRINT END
18:28 PM RECORD // Evening twilight descending over the campus trees as the 24-hour development marathon officially concluded. 2026-10-17 • 18:28

Many hackathon projects live and die inside a temporary GitHub repository, abandoned the moment the certificates are distributed. WildID is not one of them.

The challenge of individual wildlife re-identification did not conclude when the timer hit zero. If anything, the event crystallized the specific technical problems we must tackle next:

1. Curating Authentic Camera-Trap Training Corpora

In response to the judges' guidance, our primary focus is assembling an open-source benchmark dataset of real Bengal tiger camera-trap imagery across diverse seasons, night-vision infrared conditions, and partial occlusions.

2. Automated Bulk Image Ingestion Engine

Our hackathon prototype required manually selecting and submitting images. Real reserve monitoring produces tens of thousands of SD card captures monthly. We are architecting a background batch worker to ingest entire SD cards and catalog candidate sightings automatically.

3. Graph Neural Network Stripe Embedding

Moving from 2D pixel correlation to invariant topological graph neural networks (GNNs) that treat stripe bifurcations as mathematical nodes, achieving pose-invariant matching even when over 60% of the body is occluded by tall grass.

4. Conservation Field Partnerships (Aspirations)

Our ultimate hope is to explore testing our improved system in real field settings in collaboration with the administration of Pench National Park and the Tadoba-Andhari Tiger Reserve. These are ambitions rather than formalized partnerships, but they give our engineering purpose.

CHAPTER 11 // EPILOGUE

We came to build a project.
We left with a clearer direction.

Identifying an individual wild animal is more than an engineering benchmark. It is learning to read the living memory of the forest.

When we packed our bags at Ballarpur on Sunday morning, the campus was quiet. The banners for WILDHACK 2K26 swayed gently in the autumn breeze.

Looking back on those 24 intense hours, the value of the experience was never about claiming victory or proving that our code was flawless. It was about taking an idea we genuinely cared about, refusing to leave it on the shelf any longer, putting it through the crucible of a deadline, and having the humility to listen to criticism from people who protect tigers for a living.

We learned that artificial intelligence does not replace field biology — it serves it. When an algorithm correctly matches a tiger's flank pattern across a three-month gap, it is not just calculating a vector cosine distance. It is validating that this majestic predator is alive, traversing its territory, hunting along its rivers, and enduring in an increasingly fragmented landscape.

WildID started with a simple question: Can we teach computers to recognize an individual animal in the wild?

Today, we have a working foundation, a clear list of mistakes to fix, and the unwavering conviction to see this through.

This was our experience at WILDHACK 2K26. And this is only the beginning.
